KraftyPawz sells personalised pet products. I ran operations across three e-commerce
brands, and the piece I owned most directly was the ordering experience.

The starting problem was ordinary and specific. Personalised orders did not fit the
standard checkout. Customers needed to give us information the platform had no field
for, and every one of those orders turned into a manual back-and-forth after the sale.
That was fine at low volume but as we grew it stopped being fine.

## The decision that mattered was not to rebuild

The obvious move was a custom storefront. I did not do that.

Shopify already handled the parts that are genuinely hard and genuinely boring:
payments, tax, inventory, the checkout itself. Replacing that to solve a
personalisation problem would have meant owning a lot of surface area that was working
how we want it.

So I extended it instead. The storefront stayed on the platform, and I built custom
code for the two things it could not do: a path that captured personalisation details
as part of the order rather than after it, and an automated fulfilment workflow so
those orders stopped needing a person to route them.

The work came out of watching where orders actually stalled, not out of a feature list.
Most of what I changed was in the gap between what the customer thought they had
submitted and what we needed to fulfil it.

## The second tool, and why it never shipped

Editing product individually was slow. Titles, descriptions, prices, SEO
fields, all of it one at a time. So we built an internal tool that edited them in bulk,
with AI-generated description enhancements, on Rails against the Shopify API and the
OpenAI API.

It worked. We used it internally, and the obvious next step was to release it as a
Shopify app.

I ran the numbers instead, and the numbers said no. Storage and per-call pricing meant
the unit economics did not work at any price we thought people would pay. So it stayed
internal.

> Building it was the easy part. The decision I would make again is the one where I
> stopped.

## What I took from it

Two things, and the second one took longer to learn.

The first is that platform decisions are mostly about what you are willing to own.
Extending Shopify was not the more impressive engineering choice. It was the one that
left a part-time team maintaining the smallest amount of surface area.

The second is that a working build is not a reason to ship. The bulk editor was
finished, useful, and demoed well. None of that changes what it would have cost to run
per customer, and I would rather have that argument with myself before launch than
with a P&L after it.
