About two weeks into the role we saw a Microsoft end-of-support notice with 20 days
of support left on it. The feature it affected was customer-facing and it shipped to
the entire Windows user base.

The immediate problem was easy to describe and hard to solve in 20 days. The more
interesting problem was why nobody had seen it coming. Third-party deprecation notices
were arriving, and no team owned watching for them. Engineering assumed Product was
tracking vendor timelines. Product assumed the notices came through Engineering. So the
notice had been sitting in the open for weeks and the clock had been running the whole
time.

## Fixing the ticket was not the fix

I could have escalated the one deadline, got the work scheduled, and moved on. That
would have solved the instance and left the mechanism intact, which meant the next
notice would land the same way.

So I did both. The immediate deadline got a plan and an owner. Alongside it I drafted a
deprecation playbook and worked it through Product, Engineering, and Marketing leadership
until each group had agreed to what they owned.

The playbook answered four questions that had no answer before it:

- Who watches for third-party end-of-support notices, and where do they get logged
- What the assessment looks like, and who does it
- Who decides whether/when we rebuild, migrate, or communicate a change to customers
- When Marketing gets told, so customer messaging is not written in a panic

## What made it work

Getting three functions to agree on ownership is a different job from writing the
process down. Writing it took a day. Alignment took most of the following weeks,
and it worked because I brought each group a draft that already accounted for what they
would object to, rather than a blank page and a meeting invite. I gave leadership an oppotunity
to voice their concerns to the audience and collaborate to drive visibility.

The other thing that helped was scope. The playbook covered third-party deprecation
notices and nothing else. A broader "vendor risk process" would have been more
impressive to describe and much harder to get agreed.

> A process that names an owner at every step survives the person who wrote it. One
> that describes good intentions does not.

## What I would do differently

I would instrument it. The playbook defines what happens once a notice is found, and
finding notices is still a human remembering to look. If I were doing it again I would
push for something automated feeding the intake step, even a crude one, because the
failure mode I actually caught was detection and that is the part the playbook improves
least.

I would also have written down what it cost us. I know the deadline was met and I know
what the process looks like now. I do not have a number for how much engineering time
the scramble consumed, and that number would have made the case for the playbook to
anyone who was not in the room.
